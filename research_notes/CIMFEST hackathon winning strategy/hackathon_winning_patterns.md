# Hackathon Winning Patterns 2024–2026 (Global and Africa)

Scope note: research done 2026-11-21 with about 20 search/fetch calls. Primary sources are Anthropic's own winner posts (claude.com), Stanford Daily (TreeHacks), Google's blog (Solution Challenge), MTN.com, Cameroon Tribune and ecomatin (Orange Summer Challenge Cameroon), Africa.com (Hedera Africa), and judge or organizer write-ups (Semgrep, Builder's Guide, PostHog). ## 1. What did grand-prize winners at major recent hackathons build? Common traits

### Takeaway
First-place projects in 2024–2026 share a pattern. They solve a concrete, high-stakes problem for a named user, often one the builder knows from their own domain: permits, clinical training, eye disease, sickle-cell crises, the visually impaired. They pair that with a technical mechanism the judges can see working, such as multi-agent pipelines, verifier sub-agents, evals, hardware plus AI, or real-time systems. They also carry evidence that someone real would adopt them: pilots, a city considering the tool, accuracy against ground truth. In Africa the winners lean heavily toward health, agriculture and environment, often hardware plus app, and favour solutions that work under local constraints such as USSD/SMS or solar power.

### Cited Findings
**Anthropic / Claude Code hackathons (2026)**
- Built with Opus 4.6 (virtual, Claude Code). First place was **CrossBeam** by Mike Brown, a personal injury lawyer. It helps builders fix California housing-permit submissions and helps municipalities batch-process permits and draft correction letters. Parallel sub-agents parse documents and one agent is assigned per correction. Buena Park is considering adopting it. Brown: "I didn't write a single line of code." — [Claude blog, Opus 4.6 winners](https://claude.com/blog/meet-the-winners-of-our-built-with-opus-4-6-claude-code-hackathon)
- In the same event, second place was **Elisa**, a block-based visual IDE for kids, with a teaching engine. It was built in 30 hours, with 76 commits and over 39,000 lines of code. Third place was **PostVisit.ai**, built by a Brussels cardiologist to explain diagnoses and visit notes with physician oversight. The "Keep Thinking" prize went to **TARA** by Kyeyune Kazibwe, a roads and infrastructure specialist. TARA turns dashcam footage into an infrastructure investment appraisal with NPV, sensitivity analysis and an equity score. Feasibility studies cost $1–4M and take 9–14 months; Kazibwe: "TARA does it in five hours." Special prize: **Conductr**, a C engine compiled to WebAssembly that generates MIDI every 15 ms so the AI bandmate never interrupts playback. The post says four of the five named winners were not professional developers. — [Claude blog, Opus 4.6 winners](https://claude.com/blog/meet-the-winners-of-our-built-with-opus-4-6-claude-code-hackathon)
- Built with Opus 4.7 (virtual, about one week). A secondary source puts the field at 500 builders chosen from more than 20,000 applicants ([search summary citing claude.com/press](https://www.edtechinnovationhub.com/news/a-doctor-a-carpenter-and-a-teacher-win-anthropics-global-opus-47-hackathon)). First place was **Medkit** by Bedirhan Keskin, a physician-turned-engineer. It is a gamified clinic where residents diagnose simulated patients, and an *agentic grader scores each encounter against published clinical guidelines*. Three Istanbul medical faculties and a pharma company are preparing pilots. Second place was **Wrench Board**, an agent that reads schematics and boardviews, tells the technician which pad to probe, and updates its diagnosis from the measurements. Third place was **Maieutic**, an IDE that makes students write a spec first, withholds autocomplete, and compares the final code to the spec. Other prizes: **MaestrIA**, where a knowledge file built from interviews with a master carpenter raised the *eval score from 74% to 81%* ("the eval, not my intuition"), and **ARIA**, where five managed agents monitor factory signals and generate work orders. The ARIA team "spent their second day planning." — [Claude blog, Opus 4.7 winners](https://www.claude.com/blog/meet-the-winners-of-built-with-opus-4-7-claude-code-hackathon)
- Opus 4.8 Build Day (San Francisco, June 13, 12 hours, 310 builders from more than 1,500 applicants). First place was **Tekton**, which reconstructs Tang Dynasty architecture in 3D across 339 construction stages. Every component traces to documented evidence, and *independent verifier sub-agents graded each reconstruction, with all 20 tests passing*. The full building was not finished, and it still won. Second place was **Sim Francisco**: 10,000 synthetic residents built from Census data, which forecast the 2024 SF vote at 81.3% Democratic against an actual 83.8%. A clustering step cut inference cost 10–100x. Third place was **Custom Universe**, which turns one photo into an editable 3D object to generate synthetic data for robotics labs. — [Claude blog, Opus 4.8 Build Day](https://claude.com/blog/meet-the-winners-of-our-claude-opus-4-8-build-day-hackathon)

### Inferences
- **Domain insider plus agentic depth wins AI hackathons.** All three Anthropic first places (CrossBeam, Medkit, Tekton) combine deep knowledge of a real workflow with a visible multi-agent or verifier architecture: one agent per correction, an agentic grader against guidelines, verifier sub-agents with tests.
- **Verification is the new "technical complexity."** Several winners show how they know the AI is right: evals (MaestrIA, 74% to 81%), tests passing (Tekton, 20/20), ground truth (Sim Francisco, 81.3% vs 83.8%), citations to sources (Tekton). For an AI-built project this is the most persuasive form of system-design depth.
- **Hard numbers on the size of the problem** ($1–4M and 9–14 months cut to 5 hours) and **adoption signals** (a city considering it, pilots) recur in the winner write-ups.
- **Physical and real-world interaction** (cane, robot, dashcam, NFC, Kidjamo's device) is over-represented among grand prizes at in-person events.
- **African winners skew toward health, agriculture, environment and inclusion**, often device plus app or USSD/SMS. Low-connectivity design and local relevance (sickle cell, forests, livestock, water) appear to be core judging values. For a Cameroonian event such as CIMFEST, a locally grounded problem plus credible tech plus a scalability or commercial story matches the pattern of Orange Summer Challenge and PachiPanda winners.
- A project does not need to be finished. Tekton won without completing its cathedral; judges reward a working core plus a clear path forward.

- I found no published MTN-specific hackathon (as opposed to PachiPanda) winner write-up.

## 2. How do judges score? Rubrics and judge write-ups

### Takeaway
Rubrics cluster around innovation, technical execution, impact, presentation and design/UX, typically weighted about 25/25/20/15/15. In practice, judges reward clarity, a reliable working demo, a real problem, scope discipline and one "wow" moment. Some explicitly ignore code quality and line counts. With AI tools raising the floor, judges increasingly separate projects on product sense and problem framing rather than raw build volume.

### Cited Findings
- Example weights: Innovation 25%, Technical execution 25%, Impact 20%, Presentation 15%, Design/UX 15%. Judges look for a concrete problem (ideally lived experience), a working demo where "reliability matters more than complexity", and a clear explanation: "If the judges don't understand what you built, they can't score it." They look for a wow moment and for scope discipline: "Knowing what to leave out is a skill, and judges recognize it." They mostly ignore code quality, line counts, feature completeness, decorative animation and hours worked. The guide also says to reverse-engineer the build from the rubric and to target sponsor tracks, which are often less competitive. — [Builder's Guide Ch. 15: How judging works](https://intercom.help/builderbase/en/articles/14836129-builder-s-guide-ch-15-how-judging-works)
- Judges' backgrounds shape their scoring: investors weigh business viability, engineers technical depth, designers UX. — [Builder's Guide](https://intercom.help/builderbase/en/articles/14836129-builder-s-guide-ch-15-how-judging-works)
- TreeHacks 2026 criteria: creativity, technological complexity, social impact. — [Stanford Daily](https://stanforddaily.com/2026/02/15/12th-annual-treehacks/)
- ETHGlobal leads with "technicality" (problem complexity and sophistication of the solution) and uses an async screen before live finalist judging of the top ~20%. — [ETHGlobal Unite details](https://ethglobal.com/events/unite/info/details) (via search summary)
- Some rubrics state that judges may ultimately decide on "gut feeling". The DiamondHacks rubric explicitly does not score code quality. — search summary of [DiamondHacks criteria](https://github.com/annaowens/DiamondHacks21/blob/master/Judging%20Criteria.md) and related rubric pages
- Semgrep's co-CTO, who has judged more than a dozen hackathons over 15 years, wrote in 2026: "Every project was a hit". No teams relied on slides and none crashed, so judges now struggle to pick among strong demos. "The best projects weren't the ones that built the most." Winners could state the problem, explain why existing solutions fall short, and describe their solution in one sentence. He concludes that product sense now matters more than technical execution. This is anecdotal, from two internal events. — [Semgrep blog, 2026](https://semgrep.dev/blog/2026/the-ai-hackathon-effect)
- JetBrains judges (2026): a strong project with a confusing demo loses to a simpler one the judges understand. Run the pitch aloud and time it at least once. — [JetBrains blog](https://blog.jetbrains.com/ai/2026/06/how-to-win-a-hackathon-notes-from-the-judging-table/) (search snippet only; page returned 403)
- Novelty matters relative to existing tools. Judges marked down a working accessibility app because its features are becoming default in common tools. — [PostHog](https://posthog.com/blog/elevenlabs-hackathon)
- African corporate challenges (PachiPanda, Hedera Africa) frame selection around innovation, execution, real-world impact, commercial viability and scalability. — [MTN.com](https://www.mtn.com/cameroons-arnaud-njita-named-2025-mtn-africa-pachipanda-winner-for-innovative-eco-solution/); [Africa.com](https://africa.com/happening/2025-hedera-africa-hackathon-announces-winners-officially-becomes-the-largest-web3-hackathon-globally)

### Inferences
- Since AI tools make polished UIs and working code common, the remaining differentiators are problem selection, framing, a visible technical mechanism (architecture and verification) and a crisp narrative.
- To win first rather than place, a team should score highly on every rubric line rather than max out one. In practice, budget explicit time for presentation (15% weight), which is often neglected.
- Panels at African telco and corporate events (Orange, MTN) likely include business and incubation stakeholders, so a viability or business-model slide is more important there than at TreeHacks-style events.

### Gaps
- I found no first-hand judge X/Twitter threads (searching social media was not practical).
- I found no quantitative data linking rubric scores to final placement.

## 3. How are teams using AI coding tools perceived, and how do winners present AI-built projects credibly?

### Takeaway
At AI-native events (Anthropic, ElevenLabs, vibe-coding hackathons), openly building with AI is expected, and winners even celebrate "I didn't write a single line of code." Credibility comes from showing that the human contributed domain insight, planning and verification (evals, tests, verifier agents, cited sources), not from hiding the AI. Some traditional events (ETHGlobal) disqualify submissions that "rely entirely on AI without meaningful contributions" from finalist prizes. Teams should check the rules and be ready to explain their own design decisions.

### Cited Findings
- CrossBeam's winner said he "didn't write a single line of code", and four of the five named Opus 4.6 winners were not professional developers. Elisa was shipped with 39,000+ lines of code and 76 commits in 30 hours. — [Claude blog 4.6](https://claude.com/blog/meet-the-winners-of-our-built-with-opus-4-6-claude-code-hackathon)
- Opus 4.7 winners' practices included planning before coding (ARIA spent a day planning, Maieutic is spec-first) and building evals before features (MaestrIA). "You can't prompt your way to knowing what a maintenance technician actually looks at." — [Claude blog 4.7](https://www.claude.com/blog/meet-the-winners-of-built-with-opus-4-7-claude-code-hackathon)
- Tekton's credibility came from verifier sub-agents, 20/20 tests, and every component traced to evidence. — [Claude blog 4.8](https://claude.com/blog/meet-the-winners-of-our-claude-opus-4-8-build-day-hackathon)
- ETHGlobal Unite DeFi: "submissions that rely entirely on AI without meaningful contributions from team members may not be eligible for partner prizes or finalist consideration." — [ETHGlobal Unite](https://ethglobal.com/events/unite/info/details) (via search summary)
- The WashU Law Vibe Coding Challenge scored "responsible AI use" as an explicit criterion, alongside practical utility and execution and clarity. — [WashU Law](https://law.washu.edu/news/washu-law-announces-winners-of-international-vibe-coding-challenge/) (search summary)
- An ICSE SEET 2026 study of a one-day vibe-coding hackathon found that technical expertise still helped: spotting problems in code, keeping the solution aligned with goals, and choosing tools. — [ICSE SEET 2026 paper](https://eprints.soton.ac.uk/510996/1/ICSE_SEET_2026_VibeCoding.pdf) (search summary)
- One ElevenLabs hacker said the AI builder (Lovable) saved about 20 hours. The time went to user testing and iteration, which made the product better. — [PostHog](https://posthog.com/blog/elevenlabs-hackathon)
- With AI, 24-hour output "feels like a real prototype." Mocked interfaces and hardcoded data are less accepted because judges expect real data. — [Semgrep](https://semgrep.dev/blog/2026/the-ai-hackathon-effect)

### Inferences
- A credible way to present an AI-built project: (1) an architecture diagram showing agents, data flow and failure handling; (2) an eval harness or test suite with a visible pass rate or before/after metric; (3) a live demo on real data, not mocks; (4) a sentence on what the humans decided (domain rules, scope cuts, trade-offs); (5) a public repo with a CLAUDE.md, spec or plan and a commit history. Items 1–4 follow directly from the winner examples above. Item 5 is my extrapolation.
- Because AI raises everyone's floor, raw feature count no longer impresses. Depth of design, verification and a domain insight that the AI could not supply are what separate first place.
- Be prepared for the Q&A question "What did you build vs. the AI?" At events with ETHGlobal-style rules this question decides eligibility.

### Gaps
- I found no survey or study directly measuring judge bias for or against AI-assisted entries.
- I found nothing on how African hackathon juries (Orange, MTN) view AI-generated code. I could find no stated policies.

## 4. Demo and pitch tactics

### Takeaway
Lead with a human problem grounded in a real story or data, show one reliable live "wow" moment, and keep the pitch tight: ETHGlobal gives about 4 minutes. Assign separate speaker and demo-driver roles, rehearse aloud with a timer, and keep a recorded backup video for failures. Leave build struggles out of the pitch.

### Cited Findings
- Ground the problem in a real story. RoadMate's deck showed a photo from a real near-accident, and the idea came from the team's own circle. Memorable, playful presentation helped: a talking plush dinosaur drew interest despite exposed internals. — [PostHog](https://posthog.com/blog/elevenlabs-hackathon)
- A "wow" moment can come from concept, storytelling or UX rather than technical depth. Name your target users and ground the problem in data or personal experience. Practice, because presentation can decide first versus third. — [Builder's Guide](https://intercom.help/builderbase/en/articles/14836129-builder-s-guide-ch-15-how-judging-works)
- Be able to state the problem, why existing solutions fall short, and the solution in one sentence. — [Semgrep](https://semgrep.dev/blog/2026/the-ai-hackathon-effect)
- The pitch is the most important part of a hackathon. Assign one speaker and one demo driver, and leave out the problems you hit while building. — [Salesforce Developers blog (2014, older)](https://developer.salesforce.com/blogs/2014/07/deliver-winning-pitch-hackathons) (search summary)
- Record a demo video in case of a live failure. — [Cloudinary on dev.to](https://dev.to/cloudinary/how-to-win-a-hackathon-1377); [Level Up Coding](https://levelup.gitconnected.com/how-to-win-a-hackathon-ee740c6d47db) (search summaries)
- ETHGlobal pitch-plus-demo rounds are about 4 minutes, so structure the demo around the criteria. One ETHGlobal Taipei 2025 judge advised discussing your idea with judges before building. — [TechFlow/MEXC guide](https://www.mexc.com/news/1038) (secondary)
- Winner write-ups lead with quantified before/after claims: weeks to 5 hours (TARA), forecast 81.3% vs actual 83.8% (Sim Francisco), eval 74% to 81% (MaestrIA). — [Claude 4.6](https://claude.com/blog/meet-the-winners-of-our-built-with-opus-4-6-claude-code-hackathon); [Claude 4.8](https://claude.com/blog/meet-the-winners-of-our-claude-opus-4-8-build-day-hackathon); [Claude 4.7](https://www.claude.com/blog/meet-the-winners-of-built-with-opus-4-7-claude-code-hackathon)

### Inferences
- A suggested 3–4 minute structure, drawn from the sources: a real person or story (20s), the stakes in numbers (20s), a one-sentence solution, a live demo with one wow moment (about 90s), a 20s architecture and verification slide, an adoption or impact signal and next steps (20s).
- For Q&A, prepare answers on: what the AI wrote versus what you designed; how you know outputs are correct (evals or tests); how it scales and what it costs; and, for African events, the business model, connectivity constraints and local partners.
- To handle failures: pre-load demo data, keep a local or offline fallback, and have the backup video queued. Narrate calmly and switch rather than debug live (standard practice; no 2024–26 source quantifies it).

### Gaps
- I found no rigorous data on optimal pitch length or on how often live demos fail.
- First-hand judge accounts of handling demo failures from 2024–26 were not retrieved.

## 5. Team organization and time management over 24–48 hours

### Takeaway
Winning teams are small (3–4), mix domain expertise with building skills, plan and scope before coding, use AI to compress scaffolding, and spend the saved time on user testing and demo polish. They freeze features before the end rather than starting new ones late.

### Cited Findings
- Teams of 3–4 combined domain expertise with technical building. AI scaffolding saved time that went to user testing and iteration. Analytics or validation came too late for many teams; start it once the prototype is clickable. — [PostHog](https://posthog.com/blog/elevenlabs-hackathon)
- ARIA's team spent its second day planning, and Maieutic and MaestrIA used spec-first and eval-first approaches. — [Claude blog 4.7](https://www.claude.com/blog/meet-the-winners-of-built-with-opus-4-7-claude-code-hackathon)
- Small teams, fixed time boxes and no meetings produce focus. Scope cuts showed up in the strong teams' demos. — [Semgrep](https://semgrep.dev/blog/2026/the-ai-hackathon-effect)
- Scope discipline is visible to judges. — [Builder's Guide](https://intercom.help/builderbase/en/articles/14836129-builder-s-guide-ch-15-how-judging-works)
- A Smart India Hackathon winner's retrospective says judges mostly care whether the demo works. A teammate stopped a late new feature, which saved the team. — [Reskilll blog](https://blogs.reskilll.com/?p=449) (search summary)
- TreeHacks allows teams of up to 4. — [Stanford Daily 2025](https://stanforddaily.com/2025/02/18/treehacks-awards-200000-in-prizes-to-students-from-around-the-world/)
- Solo or duo builders can win AI hackathons: CrossBeam, Medkit and Elisa were individual efforts, and Tekton and Sim Francisco were duos. — [Claude blogs](https://claude.com/blog/meet-the-winners-of-our-built-with-opus-4-6-claude-code-hackathon)

### Inferences
- A suggested 24–48h plan for an AI-assisted team: hours 0–3 for problem validation, rubric mapping, spec, architecture and CLAUDE.md; then a core vertical slice end-to-end with real data; then evals and tests plus a verifier step; a feature freeze at about T-6h (24h event) or T-10h (48h); and the remaining time on demo script, backup video, deck and Q&A drills.
- Suggested roles: a domain/story lead who pitches; a systems architect who directs the agents and owns integration; a demo/UX and verification owner. Run Claude Code sessions in parallel on separate modules, with one person merging.

### Gaps
- I found no 2024–26 data on sleep or break practices, or on the hour-by-hour schedules of specific grand-prize teams.
